Privacy is rarely just a technical setting or an abstract legal right; it is the foundation of personal safety, dignity, and agency in digital spaces. Within our lab, the Privacy Risks, Harms, and Protections theme investigates how the erosion of privacy fuels online harms—and how we can design better systems to stop it. Aligned with our core mission to understand and mitigate online harms through novel, user-centred technical governance mechanisms, this theme explores what happens when personal data is exposed, exploited, or poorly governed, and builds practical frameworks that prioritise accountability, support, and restoration over punitive reactions.


Online harms seldom occur in a vacuum. Time and again, harassment, stalking, coercion, and discrimination begin with—or are severely exacerbated by—failures in data privacy. When sensitive personal information is leaked, sold through opaque data broker ecosystems, or inferred by invasive algorithms, it hands bad actors the leverage they need to target vulnerable individuals. A breach of reproductive health data can expose users to legal and social persecution; an unguarded data trail can enable doxxing and sustained abuse; intimate disclosures shared with an automated system can be weaponised or commodified without a user’s meaningful consent. Traditional approaches to digital privacy often place the burden of protection squarely on the individual, expecting users to navigate labyrinthine privacy policies and toggle endless settings. When harms do occur, conventional governance models tend to focus on reactive, punitive measures after the damage is already done. Our research challenges both paradigms. We treat privacy not as an individual compliance chore, but as a relational and systemic condition necessary for collective safety online.
